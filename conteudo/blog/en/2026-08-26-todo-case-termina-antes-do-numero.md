I put my own portfolio through an audit calibrated as a **hiring manager's first
screen**, for a mid-level Product/UX Designer role. Every external link tested,
the content read the way the site serves it.

I'll start with the good part, because it exists and ignoring it would be
another kind of dishonesty.

**What passed:**

- **the discipline about evidence** — the funnel, the heatmap with 182 clicks on
  "close the pop-up" against 5 on "buy", the search that returns a mousepad to
  somebody who typed "mouse";
- **the "what I turned down" section**, which names trade-offs instead of listing
  deliverables;
- **the writing in the first person**, with honest scope, no "we" hiding who did
  what.

Now the part that matters.

## Not one case closes with a result measured in production

Four cases, four endings:

| Case | How it ends |
|---|---|
| PCYES | "goes into production in October" |
| Locarmais | "I have no formal measurement" |
| ODEX | "live in time for the trade show" |
| Oderço | from three systems to two, and that is a side effect |

It is **the same ending, four times.**

The reader finishes convinced that I think well and that I really build. And
without a single numerical proof that what I built moved a business number.

::destaque
Being honest about a missing number is not a substitute for having one.
::

That sentence cost me an afternoon of bad mood, and it is right.

I had been treating "I'd rather not present a number that doesn't exist" as if it
were the virtue that solved the problem. **It is a virtue, and it doesn't solve
it.** It keeps me from lying. Whoever is reading still doesn't get the answer.

## The question the screen is actually asking

Every UX portfolio answers two questions well:

1. can this person think?
2. can this person design?

Those are easy to answer with prose and with screenshots, which is why almost
everybody answers them. The third one is what decides the band:

> Does this person's work reach production and move a number?

That one doesn't get answered with prose. Writing it better doesn't help.

Either there is a measured before and a measured after, or the answer stays
open — and the person reading fills the gap with the cheapest assumption.

::margem
This holds for people starting out too. "I don't have access to metrics" is true
in a lot of internships and a lot of freelance work. Even there something is
countable: how many support tickets about that screen before and after, how many
steps the flow had, how long the task took.
::

## The detail that stings

The checkout fix I made **before** the redesign is in production on the store
that sells today. And I have GA4, invoices and Clarity in hand.

Checkout completion and drop-off by step, before and after: **that number already
exists in my own data.** It just was never pulled.

One paragraph, with the rate before and the rate after, would turn the strongest
chapter on the site from a well-argued promise into a measured result. That is
not a month of work. It is one query and one afternoon.

Finding that out is worse than finding a gap. A gap you explain with context.
Here the material was sitting right next to me, and I spent months improving the
narrative around a space I could have filled.

## What can be done while the number isn't there

In the cases where formal measurement genuinely doesn't exist, change the ending:
instead of closing on the absence, **close on the decision that now stands.**

At Locarmais I have three verifiable behaviours from after the module went in:

- the finance team **stopped keeping the parallel spreadsheet**;
- **stopped opening tickets** with the development team just to pull a report;
- and reconciliation **came off a paid platform** and moved back in house.

A habit that dies is the hardest result to get, and it is a good deal stronger
than it sounds written out like that. What's missing is quantifying what can be
quantified: how many entries a day, how many acquirers, how many parallel
spreadsheets there used to be.

"Replaced a paid tool" gets twice as strong with the name of the cost that left
the books.

## What I'd do differently from the very first case

**Write down the before number on the day the project starts.**

It sounds obvious put like that. In practice nobody does it, because at the start
of a project the before number looks like nothing more than a portrait of the
problem you already understood.

It is half the proof. And the other half only exists if that half was kept.

Six months later, when the case is being written, nobody goes back to find out
what the completion rate was in March.

I didn't go back. That is why four chapters of this site end on a dotted line.
